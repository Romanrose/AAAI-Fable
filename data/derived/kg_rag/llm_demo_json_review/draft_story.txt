In the garden workshop, a careful caretaker named Mira prepared a sunlit plot, ensuring the soil was rich and the leaves of her young plants were green with chlorophyll. She knew that without light, no true growth could occur.

Each morning, the plants stretched toward the sun, using its energy to turn water and air into starch—their visible food. Nearby, a compost pile breathed out warmth, a reminder that all living things consume and release, but only the green leaves could capture light and build anew.

One day, Mira placed a glass jar over a sprig of pondweed. Bubbles rose steadily in sunlight. But when she moved the jar to shade, the bubbles slowed. In darkness, they stopped entirely. The hidden prerequisite—light—was the key.

To test further, she set a tube of algae at different distances from a lamp. At 10 cm, sixty bubbles per minute; at 40 cm, only five. The closer the light, the more vigorous the work.

Mira smiled. The plot’s growth was genuine, sustained only by the captured light. She understood now: the process was not vague improvement, but a precise, light-driven transformation.
