In a sun-dappled garden workshop, Old Tobin prepared a plot of fine soil, checking its moisture and warmth with careful hands. He buried three seeds—one plump, one cracked, one hollow—each in identical beds. Each day, he watered them equally and watched.

After a week, the plump seed split the soil with a pale shoot. The cracked seed swelled but remained still. The hollow seed rotted. Tobin then dug up the cracked seed and placed it in a warmer, darker box with steady dampness. Within days, it too sent out a root.

“The seed’s own store fed the first push,” Tobin told his apprentice. “But without the right warmth and water, even a good seed stays asleep. The hollow one had no food inside—no amount of care could wake it.”

To test, the apprentice planted a plump seed in dry sand and another in cold clay. Neither grew. Only when both inner readiness and outer conditions met did the hidden life stir upward.

The garden flourished not by wishing, but by preparing what the seeds silently required.
