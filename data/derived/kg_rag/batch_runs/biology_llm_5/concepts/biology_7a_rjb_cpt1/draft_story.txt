In a sunlit garden workshop, a careful caretaker named Elara prepared a plot of rich soil, ensuring it had water, air, and warmth. She planted a single seed, then tended it with precise tools and routines—watering at dawn, weeding by noon, and checking the soil’s breath each evening. Nearby, a patch of moss grew on a stone, and a vine crept over the fence, competing for light and nutrients.

Days passed, and a green shoot emerged. Elara observed closely: the shoot reached toward the sun, absorbed water, and pushed out tiny leaves. To test if this growth was genuine, she gently touched the stem—it bent, then slowly straightened, responding to her touch. She then covered one leaf with a dark cloth; within hours, it paled, showing it needed light to live. Finally, she separated a small cutting and placed it in a new pot; it rooted and grew, proving it could reproduce.

The system stabilized. Elara understood: the seed was a living thing—it needed nutrition, breathed through its leaves, responded to stimuli, grew, and reproduced. The moss and vine were also alive, but the stone was not. The visible changes revealed that life required all these hidden conditions to be coordinated.
